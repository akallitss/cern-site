How det2 is bent: reading a detector's shape from its pulses

A step-by-step, picture-first explanation of how the cosmic-ray pulses of the P2 Micromegas det2 reveal its shape: a drift gap that is 3.3 mm in the centre and up to 7 mm along one side, an amplification gap that varies by a few microns, and what both do to the signal and to the lost muons.

Analysis 2026-11-27 P2, det2, micromegas, drift gap, amplification gap, waveform, timing, cosmics

In one paragraph

A Micromegas has two gas gaps stacked on top of each other: a thick drift gap (4 mm by design) where the muon leaves its charge, and a thin amplification gap (0.15 mm) where that charge is multiplied. If the chamber is not flat, either gap can change from place to place, and both change the signal. With hits alone (one time and one amplitude per pad) the two effects look the same. With the full waveforms they can be told apart. Doing that on the cosmic-bench data of det2 (24 and 25 November, mesh 430 V, drift 730 V) shows a chamber whose drift gap is far from uniform, and it explains where the muons are lost.

1 · The detector

The chamber in one picture

The life of one signal, in four steps:

  1. A cosmic muon crosses the drift gap and frees electrons all along its path, about nine per millimetre in small clumps.
  2. The electrons drift down to the mesh. Those born right above the mesh arrive at once; those born under the window need about 128 ns. So the charge arrives spread over the time it takes to cross the gap.
  3. In the amplification gap each electron triggers an avalanche (a gain of several thousand). The ions it creates drift back to the mesh, which takes a fixed time set by that gap.
  4. The electronics shape the pad current into a pulse and sample it every 60 ns.

Two things in this picture depend on the shape of the chamber. A thicker drift gap means more electrons and a longer arrival. A thicker amplification gap means a weaker field, so a lower gain, and a longer way home for the ions.

2 · The problem

Why the two gaps hide behind each other

The pulse on a pad is three simple shapes folded into one another. Each one adds its own length to the pulse, and a later, longer pulse does not say which of them grew.

What breaks the tie. The small print under the boxes. The blue box is rebuilt by every muon from wherever its electrons happened to be, so its timing scatters from pulse to pulse. The orange box and the electronics are identical for every pulse. And only the blue box depends on which part of the gap the charge came from. The three tricks below use exactly these two facts.

4 · Trick 1

How much the pulses scatter

Take one pad and many muons. Each muon's electrons sit at different heights, so each pulse comes a little earlier or later. The size of that scatter is a fixed fraction of the time needed to cross the drift gap, and nothing else enters: the ion drift and the electronics are the same every time.

On det2 the scatter is about 21 ns over most of the chamber. Along the left side it reaches 32 ns, and in the centre it is 20 ns. The points on the right are groups of pads sorted by their drift time (measured independently, see the next sections): the scatter follows the drift time in proportion.

Detail that matters: only muons that fire a single pad are used. When a slanted track shares its charge between two pads, each pad sees only part of the gap, and that adds scatter of its own (it is trick 3).

5 · Trick 2

Same start, longer stretch

A longer cable would move the whole pulse to the right. That is not what the late zone of det2 does. Its average pulse starts at the same moment as everywhere else and then rises more slowly and peaks about 40 ns later. The electrons born next to the mesh arrive on time; the last ones, from under the window, arrive much later. That is a longer way to drift.

6 · Trick 3

Two pads under one track

A slanted muon that crosses the border between two pads splits its path in two: the lower half over one pad, the upper half over the other. The upper charge drifts longer, so that pad fires later. For a clean split the difference is half the full drift time, whatever the shape of the pulse.

On det2 this gives a textbook S-curve. Sorting the pad pairs by the drift gap of their pads, the plateau goes from about 40 ns in the thin centre to 50 ns in the nominal zone and 70 ns on the ridge. After a correction for diffusion and thresholds, that is 118 ± 3 ns in the nominal zone and 166 ± 5 ns on the ridge (129 and 174 ns if the diffusion of dry gas is assumed; see the box in section 8).

The sign of the curve also says which way is up: the charge at larger height arrives later, so the window is on top and the board below.

Thicker gap or slower drift? A longer drift time could also be slower electrons in the same 4 mm. Three observations say the gap is really thicker on the ridge: there is 1.7 times more charge and the charge distribution is narrower (more electrons), the amplitude-versus-voltage curve is stretched by the same factor (weaker field), and slanted tracks fire two pads twice as often (19 % against 8 %), because in a thicker gap a slanted track travels further sideways.

The pattern is the same on both days (the chamber was moved in between) and constant over the 7.6 hours of the long run. It is a fixed shape, not something that drifts.

One subtlety: the gas is not the textbook gas, and that sets the millimetre scale

Turning a drift time into millimetres needs the drift speed. The dry-gas calculation (Magboltz, Ar/isobutane 95/5) matches det2 above 600 V/cm, but below that the electrons are clearly slower, down to 26 µm/ns instead of 41 at 250 V/cm. On the ridge the field is only about 480 V/cm, so this matters. The map therefore uses the speed that the chamber itself shows in its drift scan, in the zones where the gap is nominal. With the textbook speed the ridge would come out at 8.2 mm instead of 7.2 mm.

Why is the gas slow? A scan of gas mixtures in Magboltz gives a clear answer: about 0.7 % of water vapour reproduces the whole drift scan, from 62 to 1312 V/cm, with one gas and one gap. Dry gas, extra air and extra isobutane do not. Water through the thin foil window is plausible at low gas flow; a hygrometer on the exhaust would confirm it.

Could a thicker gap do the same as the water? No. A thicker gap only stretches the curve; the data need the drift speed to keep rising up to 450 V/cm, which dry gas never does (no gap between 3 and 6 mm comes close). What the scan alone cannot decide is the mix: a little more water with a thinner gap fits as well as a little less water with a thicker one (0.9 % at 3.5 mm, 0.75 % at 4.0 mm, 0.5 % at 5.0 mm).

Trick 3 breaks the tie, up to a point. Along that line of solutions the drift time at the operating point changes from 96 to 132 ns, and the pad pairs measure it directly. One correction is needed: sideways diffusion blurs the split between the two pads, and diffusion is itself smaller in wet gas. Using the wet-gas diffusion (which the shape of the S-curve also prefers) the main zone gives 118 ± 3 ns. Together with the scan: about 0.6 % water and a main-zone gap of 4.4 mm (4.1–4.7). The same pad-pair measurement repeated at every point of the drift scan of the other day, with hits only, gives 0.7 % and 4.0 mm.

So the millimetres on this page, which use the dry-gas speed, are probably about 10 % too small (between 4 and 19 %): main zone 4.4 mm, thin centre 3.7 mm, ridge up to 8 mm. The shape of the map and all ratios do not change. A hygrometer on the gas exhaust, or the capacitance between window and mesh, would settle the last ±0.3 mm.

9 · Geometry

Which face moved?

A gap can open because the window lifts, because the board sinks, or both. Timing cannot tell: it measures the distance between them. The reference tracker can. Its straight tracks say at which height the chamber sees the muon, and that height is the middle of the gap.

The measured height does not move. Between the nominal pads and the thickest ones the gap opens by 3 mm and the middle shifts by −0.14 ± 0.26 mm. A window-only bulge would have lifted it by 1.5 mm, a board-only sag would have lowered it by 1.5 mm. Each is excluded at more than five standard deviations.

So on the ridge both faces stand apart: the window about 1.4 mm too high and the board about 1.7 mm too low (± 0.26 mm). The picture people had in mind, a foil sagging on a flat board, is not what happens there.

In the thin centre the same test cannot decide: the gap change is small (−0.6 mm) and both pictures fit. What causes the ridge is a question for the bench. A straight edge across the left side, on both faces, would show it directly.

10 · The other gap

The second ingredient: the amplification gap

Now that the drift gap is known pad by pad, its effect on the pulse height can be removed: more gap means more electrons (in proportion), a slightly different collection at the local field, and a slightly lower peak because the charge arrives over a longer time. What is left is the gain of the amplification gap itself.

Select Gain after removing the gap on the map above: the gain is low at the top of the fan and high in the two lower corners, and it no longer follows the drift gap at all.

Is that gain map really the amplification gap? The test is the ion tail. From tricks 1 and 2 we have two drift times per zone: one from the scatter (blind to the ions) and one from the average pulse (which also grows when the ion tail grows). Their difference is the ion part. It is longer where the gain is lower: −16 ± 5 ns for every factor e (2.7) of gain, of the size expected if most of the gain map is the gap.

If so, the amplification gap varies by about 10 µm rms around 150 µm (−21 to +16 µm). The X-ray (Fe55) data of July, taken on other electronics channels, show the same gain map (correlation 0.81) and the same link between gain and rise time.

11 · Consequences

What it does to the muons

Everything that depends on the amount of charge, on the field or on how far a slanted track travels sideways changes with the local drift gap. Three examples, with the pads sorted into four groups from the thin centre to the ridge.

Why the pillars matter less in a thick gap: there are more electrons to begin with, they diffuse more on the longer way down, and a slanted track spreads them over a wider strip, so fewer of them end up on a pillar.

Both gaps count. In a fit to all 40,513 tracks, the chance of losing a muon falls with the local drift gap and, separately, with the local gain: a factor e of gain divides the odds by three, a gap 1.5 times thicker divides them by two. Within the nominal-gap pads alone, the lost fraction is 4.7 %, 3.2 % and 2.1 % for the low, middle and high third of the gain.

For the design. A thicker drift gap buys efficiency (0.9 % lost at 6.3 mm against 3.4 % at 4.0 mm) and pays in time resolution (32 ns against 21 ns), because the scatter of the arrival times grows with the gap. And any efficiency, gain or timing number of det2 quoted as one value is an average over a chamber that is not flat.

12 · Design

Choosing the drift gap: time spread against lost muons

det2 is an accidental experiment: one chamber with drift gaps from 3.3 to 7 mm, scanned in drift voltage and in gain. That is what is needed to predict what a chamber built with another gap would do. The scans matter here: in a single run a thicker gap also means a weaker field, and only the voltage scan tells the two apart.

Lost muons. Every reference track is one trial: hit or no hit. All 116,712 tracks of the long run and of 28 scan points (drift field above 250 V/cm) are described by one model with two ways of losing a muon:

  • signal too small: depends on the collected charge, that is gain times gap;
  • pillar shadow: depends on the charge and the field too, and fades as the track leans, because a slanted track spreads its charge sideways by gap × tan(angle). The fitted reach is : at 4 mm the shadow falls by a factor e every 12°, at 6 mm every 8°.

The model reproduces the measured loss in , and it finds by itself how fast the gain rises with mesh voltage (3.2 % per volt; the pulse heights say 3.3 %).

Time spread. It is the drift time across the gap times a factor that grows slowly with the angle (0.16 for vertical tracks, 0.185 at 15–20°), the same for all four gap groups. A thicker gap at the same field is simply a proportionally longer drift.

What a chamber with a given gap would do. Choose the drift field. "Fixed field" means the drift voltage is raised with the gap, as one would do in a new design; "det2 as built" keeps 300 V across whatever gap there is.

3.5 mm4.0 mm5.0 mm6.0 mm

How to read it. At 750 V/cm, going from 4 to 5 mm removes 40 to 50 % of the lost muons at every angle (vertical: 5.3 → 3.1 %; at 15–20°: 1.7 → 0.8 %) and costs 5 ns of time spread (20–24 → 25–30 ns). Each further millimetre does roughly the same again. For tracks that lean more than 15° the losses are already near 1 % at 4 mm, so a thicker gap buys little there and still costs the full 25 % in time.

At 4 mm and vertical incidence, 4.9 of the 5.3 % are the pillar shadow and 0.4 % the threshold. More gain shrinks both; the pillar pattern only touches the first.

What these numbers assume. They are for det2 as it is: 0.8 mm pillars on a 3 mm lattice, its threshold, its median gain, its gas. The time spread is the single leading pad and includes the trigger scintillators. Between the two days the same voltages gave different losses (the scan day had 25 % less shadow), so absolute losses carry that much uncertainty; the dependence on gap and angle is what transfers. Gaps are on the same scale as the map above (see the box in section 8).

13 · Context

Other chambers, and what is still open

The same recipe was run once, quickly, on two other chambers. It tells them apart, which is a good sign for the method.

chamberwhat the pulses say
det2Dominated by the drift gap: scatter and average pulse agree, 113 to 182 ns.
det1The opposite. The scatter is nearly flat (107–125 ns) while the average pulse length varies by a factor two and follows the gain. Its timing map is the amplification gap.
det5Needs its own study (low gain). Its pad pairs differ by 80–86 ns where det2's differ by 55–59 ns: either its drift gap is 5 to 6.5 mm, or its gas was different.

Still open

  • What holds the two faces 3 mm apart along the left side. The run log calls this chamber "bulked with misaligned wall"; the data cannot say whether that is the cause.
  • Window or board in the thin centre.
  • The water content of the gas (about 0.6 % from the fits; not yet measured directly). It fixes the common millimetre scale of the map.
  • Why the pulse height drops at very low drift field. It is not electrons lost on the way: the timing rules that out.
  • Why the gain contrast is 1.5 times larger now than in the July X-ray map.
