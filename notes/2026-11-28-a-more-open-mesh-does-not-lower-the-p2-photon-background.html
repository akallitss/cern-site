A more open mesh does not lower the P2 photon background

Going from an 18/50 to an 18/60 mesh removes 13 % of the steel, but the photon hit rate stays the same. The electrons the mesh no longer emits are replaced by electrons from the copper pads, which now get through the bigger holes. Geant4 simulation with the P2Sim photon spectrum, energies and angles, 28 November 2026.

Simulation 2026-11-28 P2, Micromegas, mesh, photon background, Geant4, P2Sim

The question

The P2 Micromegas see a large photon background: about 47 GHz of photons per MM0 layer, mostly 40–150 keV. Most of the photons that leave a signal convert in a solid next to the drift gas, not in the gas itself. The conversion electron escapes into the gas and ionises it. The stainless-steel micromesh is one of those solids, so the proposal was:

Use a more open mesh, 18 µm wires with 60 µm openings instead of 50 µm. That is 13 % less steel, so it should give fewer photon conversions and less background.

We simulated it. The background does not go down.

Why: copper electrons replace steel electrons

The mesh sits 150 µm above the copper readout pads. A photon that crosses the drift gas reaches the mesh first, and through the holes of the mesh it reaches the copper pads below. Both metals photo-absorb or Compton scatter it, and both emit electrons. Steel (Fe, Z = 26) and copper (Z = 29) emit a similar number of electrons per unit area.

Counting where every hit starts gives the picture below. For the open 18/60 mesh, the hits from the steel change by −2.8 MHz and the hits from the copper by +3.0 MHz. Gas conversions and the drift cathode barely change. Even removing the steel completely changes the total by only −9.6 % in MM1 and MM2, because the copper takes over most of the steel's share. (In MM0 the change is +0.6 %, but only by coincidence: the hits above 40 keV fall by about 10 % there too, and extra hits from a 10–15 keV line in its spectrum make up for them; see below.)

The P2 photon background we used

The photons come from P2Sim (Matthieu Gailliard, BkgHistograms.root, detector test geometry MMP2_v4). For every photon entering a layer's drift gap it records the energy and the angle. We used both: the energy spectrum and the incidence angle (median 17.5° in MM0, 22 % of the flux beyond 30°).

Layer

MM0 has a sharp line at 10–15 keV: 1,442 MHz of photons, which give 46 of its 110 MHz of hits (63 % of them photons converting in the gas, which do not care about the mesh). MM1 and MM2 see only about 47 MHz in that bin, which is most of the reason MM0 has about twice their hit rate.

Multiplying the flux by the simulated probability that a photon of that energy and angle makes a hit gives the hit rate per energy, split by origin. The chart below shows what the proposed mesh changes in each energy band. In every band the steel hits (blue) go down and the copper hits (orange) go up by about the same amount, so the total (black) stays at zero within the errors (the largest deviation is +0.6 ± 0.3 MHz, 2.3σ, in the 0–20 keV band of MM0). Switch to "no steel" to see the limit. There the copper makes up for most, but not all, of the steel above 40 keV, and the 10–15 keV hits go up.

The answer does not depend on the threshold. The 18/60 to 18/50 ratio for ≥ 10 and ≥ 30 primary electrons is 1.005 ± 0.004 and 1.005 ± 0.004 in MM0. Above a few primaries a hit is a whole photo- or Compton electron track, not a single ionisation.

How far to trust this

  • Angles matter for the absolute rate, not for the ratio. Oblique photons cross more metal and more gas. Folding in the P2Sim angles raises the MM0 rate from 95.0 to 109.6 MHz (+15 %) for every mesh alike.
  • The same answer in every variant. The ratio 18/60 / 18/50 in MM0 is:
    • 1.004 ± 0.004 with angles;
    • 1.003 with normal incidence only;
    • 1.004 with angle interpolation in path length instead of angle;
    • 1.004 ± 0.003 from the first scan (normal incidence, 10–150 keV);
    • 1.005 ± 0.003 from the cruder mesh model, a uniform steel sheet of the same mass. That model has no holes, so it gets the absolute rate wrong (it hides the copper), but it gives the same ratio.
  • Statistics: 2·106 photons per job, with:
    • 4·107 per point at 40–150 keV and normal incidence;
    • 107 per point at 20°, 40° and 60° (40–300 keV), 4·106 below 40 keV;
    • 1.6·109 photons over the three meshes; 8.5·108 of them are new for this cross-check.
    The quoted errors are statistical, propagated through the fold.
  • Geometry:
    • The mesh is two crossed layers of round steel wires with real holes (Geant4, wire-resolved).
    • Not modelled: the weave, the calendering, and the mesh alloy (G4 stainless steel is used).
    • The 18/50 mesh is the measured P2 mesh. 18/60 is the candidate.
    • The readout pads, PCB, pillars, drift cathode and window are as in the P2 production geometry.
  • Physics: Geant4 with Garfield++/Heed for the primary ionisation in the gas (the P2 production settings).
  • Not covered:
    • photons that P2Sim records entering the gap from behind (through the PCB), 5.2 % of the MM0 flux;
    • photons above 1 MeV, 0.4 %;
    • incidence beyond 60°, which is taken with the 60° response, 2 % of the flux.
    None of these is expected to favour one mesh.
  • What this is not: it counts primary ionisation. Transparency, gain, timing and ion backflow change too with the mesh: transparency improves a little, the gain drops by about 15 % at fixed voltage, and ion backflow rises by about 22 %. That is a separate study, also done; ask if you want it.

Bottom line. Going from 18/50 to 18/60 changes the photon hit rate by less than 1 % in every layer: 1.004 ± 0.004 (MM0), 0.998 ± 0.005 (MM1), 0.998 ± 0.005 (MM2). The metal under the drift gas, mesh plus pads, sets the background, and a more open mesh only replaces iron electrons with copper electrons. Even the extreme limit, no mesh steel at all, would gain only about 10 % in MM1 and MM2 and nothing in MM0.
