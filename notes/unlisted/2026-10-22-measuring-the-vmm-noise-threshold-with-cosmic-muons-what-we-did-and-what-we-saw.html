Measuring the VMM noise threshold with cosmic muons: what we did and what we saw

A picture book of the first lab threshold scan on the three-chamber cosmic stand: why, how, and the first noise results per chip.

Detector R&D 2026-10-22 VMM,threshold,noise,cosmics,P2,lab
In one minute.
  • Question: how low can we set the VMM threshold before the electronics noise takes over, chip by chip? The beam test could only predict this.
  • What we did: on 22 Oct we scanned the threshold of all hybrids on the three-chamber cosmic stand, 10 steps of 2 minutes each, with the chambers at high voltage.
  • What we found: (1) three chips (VMM 4, 7, 9) throw bursts on single channels that do not depend on the threshold; (2) for the 14 chips that gave a clean curve (not 4, 6, 7, 9), the point where noise starts differs by up to 16 DAC from chip to chip, so one common threshold cannot be right; (3) a few chips show a rate that falls again at very low threshold, which we cannot yet explain.
  • Next: measure each channel's own baseline directly from the chip (calibration), then repeat the scan with efficiency measured by tag-and-probe.

1. Why measure this at all?

A VMM channel decides "there was a hit" when the pulse height passes a threshold. A low threshold catches small muon signals (good efficiency) but also starts catching electronic noise (bad). In the SPS beam data the VMM efficiency was about 85 % against 95.6 % for the DREAM readout of the same chambers, and the analysis argued the VMM threshold was set too high for the signal. That was a model built from DREAM spectra and a short bench scan; the beam never let us vary the threshold. Cosmic muons arrive at a steady, gentle rate, and in the lab we can wait for them while changing the threshold.

2. The set-up

Three chambers stacked like at the beam test (chambers det3, det2, det1 now stand where IN, MID, OUT were). Nine hybrids with two VMM chips each read them out (VMM 2–19); hybrid 0, the beam-test trigger, is not used. The chips decide for themselves when to record a hit.

The high voltage (410 V mesh, 710 V drift on all three) is fixed and not recorded by the DAQ, so we write it in the logbook by hand. The gas is Ar/isobutane 95/5 but this is not yet confirmed, and it is not the gas of the beam test, so we compare with the beam through measured spectra, never through voltages.

3. The knob: a threshold per chip, measured from its own baseline

The threshold is a number (sdt, in DAC units) written to each chip. Each chip's electronics rests at a slightly different level, its baseline. A threshold below the baseline makes the chip fire all the time. So one common value is a trap: at sdt = 200, every chip whose baseline is above 200 would flood the readout.

Estimated baselines from the July beam ADC spectra (real numbers). The shaded band is where one common threshold of 200 would be below the chip's baseline: chips 2, 5, 10, 11, 12, 14 and 19 would fire continuously. Hollow dots are extrapolated, VMM 6 has no estimate.

So the scan is written as d = threshold − baseline for each chip: d = 50 is a comfortable margin, d = 16 is already inside the noise for most chips. The baselines used are the July estimates, and that is the weak point: if they are off, d does not mean the same distance for every chip. That is exactly what the results below show.

4. What happened, in order

  1. 1
    Smoke test
    The July chip configuration at high voltage, 2 minutes: all 18 chips show up, but 1.5 GB of data.
  2. 2
    Find the flood
    Almost all hits come from three chips, from single channels: VMM 4, 9 (and later 7).
  3. 3
    Try masking
    Switched-off channels go silent, but the burst hops to other channels.
  4. 4
    Mask whole chips
    VMM 4 and 9 fully masked: the data rate falls from 635 to 5 MB/min.
  5. 5
    Scan the threshold
    10 steps, d = 50 → 16, 2 minutes each; 15 chips give a curve (VMM 7 only part way).

The path to a usable scan.

The first test at the July thresholds wrote 1.5 GB in two minutes: about 1.6 million hits per second, almost all from three chips. Looking at which channels fired showed that a single channel on VMM 4 (channel 49) and a handful on VMM 9 produced nearly everything, and the same at thresholds far above the baseline: not a threshold effect.

Real counts from the first masked test. Switching off the five hot channels of VMM 9 and the hot channel of VMM 4 did not help: the burst moved to channels 20/21 on VMM 9 and 36/38 on VMM 4. This is the "walking burst" noted in July.

Masking channel by channel therefore does not work, so for the scan VMM 4 and 9 were switched off completely (all 64 channels), and one channel on VMM 7. That cut the data rate from 635 to 5 MB/min (a factor of more than 100) and left the other chips quiet. It also means we have no threshold information for those two chips, and the cosmic tracks will not see part of two chambers. VMM 7 then started bursting on a neighbouring channel (10.9 kHz at d = 40, 489 kHz at d = 34).

5. The result: where each chip starts to fire

For each of ten threshold steps we recorded two minutes and counted the hits per chip from the start of the file.

The same information as a ladder: the step at which each chip first passes 1 kHz. The spread is 16 DAC (d = 34 for VMM 15, d = 18 for VMM 10; VMM 2 never gets there).

The cost in disk space: from 5 MB/min to 4.3 GB/min, a factor of nearly a thousand over this range. The dashed line is the early test with the hot channels on.

What this says

  • The noise does not start at the same distance above the assumed baseline for every chip, so our baselines are off, by up to ~16 DAC. Chips that fire early (VMM 15, 12, 14, 19) have a higher true baseline than assumed; VMM 2 a lower one.
  • The rise is steep: once a chip passes 1 kHz, a few DAC more takes it to hundreds of kHz. The useful window between "quiet" and "flooded" is narrow, and different for each chip.
  • At the lowest steps the total rate is so high (4 GB/min) that the numbers may be affected by the link, see the next figure.

Two chips whose rate goes down as the threshold is lowered further. Possibilities: the readout starves when the link saturates, a start-of-run effect (we only read the first 40 MB of each file), or lost packets. Not yet checked.

6. What this does not say yet

  • No efficiency yet. This was the noise side only. The signal side needs the tag-and-probe analysis below.
  • Rates come from the first 40 MB of each file; at the highest rates that covers only half a second.
  • VMM 4 and 9 (masked) and VMM 6 (fixed threshold) give no curve; VMM 7 only down to d = 34.
  • The gas is unconfirmed and the voltage readings are not logged by the DAQ.
  • This is a lab, not the beam hall: the beam-hall noise was about 100× higher, so the lab onset is the best case.

7. What comes next

A per-channel calibration reads each channel's baseline from the chip itself (an internal monitor), then adjusts a small trim per channel so that all channels start at the same level. This is what the vmmsc software of the RD51 group does; our slow-control tool does not offer the read yet.

Once the thresholds are calibrated we redo the scan and measure efficiency against the same steps.

  1. Get the monitor-ADC read working (library call exists, command line does not) and measure pedestals per channel on all hybrids.
  2. Rebuild the scan around the measured baselines, and give VMM 4, 7 and 9 a closer look (hybrid, cable, firmware?).
  3. Overnight cosmic reference run at a safe threshold: track rates, alignment, det3 health.
  4. Efficiency vs threshold by tag-and-probe, per chip, then the same with trims.
