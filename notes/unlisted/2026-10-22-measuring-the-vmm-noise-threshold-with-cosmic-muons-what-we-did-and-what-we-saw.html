Measuring the VMM noise threshold with cosmic muons: what we did and what we saw

A picture book of the first lab threshold scan on the three-chamber cosmic stand: why, how, and the first noise results per chip.

Detector R&D 2026-10-22 VMM,threshold,noise,cosmics,P2,lab
In one minute.
  • The question: how low can we set the threshold of each VMM chip before electronic noise takes over? The beam test could only guess the answer. Here, with cosmic muons, we can measure it.
  • What we did, in order: (1) a first test, which showed a flood of data from a few chips; (2) a noise scan with the chamber voltage on; (3) the same scan with the voltage off, to separate the detector from the electronics; (4) a closer look at single channels; (5) a direct measurement of the baseline of each channel, using a chip setting where a firing channel makes its neighbours report.
  • What we found: the chips do not start to be noisy at the same threshold, and this spread stays when the voltage is off, so it comes from the electronics, not the chambers. Three chips (VMM 4, 7, 9) have noisy channels that also stay with the voltage off. And each channel is noisiest right at its own baseline, so channels inside one chip differ too. The baselines we could measure directly (about half of the channels so far) are mostly 45 to 65 ADC counts, not the 38 used so far for every channel, and they differ from chip to chip.
  • What this means for the next step: one threshold per chip is not enough. We need the baseline of every channel (half is done), in threshold units (millivolts, not yet done), and then to correct each channel. Then we repeat the scan and, only then, measure efficiency.

0. The idea in one picture

A VMM channel records a hit when the pulse it sees goes above a threshold. If the threshold is high, small muon pulses are missed (low efficiency). If it is low, electronic noise also goes above it (many false hits). Move the slider to feel the trade-off:

Muon signals seen (efficiency)
Noise hits per second, relative

Cartoon with invented shapes: the point is the trade-off, not the numbers.

In the SPS beam data the VMM efficiency was about 85 % against 95.6 % for the DREAM readout of the same chambers, and the analysis said the VMM threshold was probably too high. That was a prediction: the beam gave no time to vary the threshold. In the lab we can, because cosmic muons arrive at a steady, gentle rate and we can wait.

1. The set-up

Three chambers stacked like at the beam test. Nine hybrids with two VMM chips each read them out (VMM 2 to 19). The chips decide for themselves when to record a hit; there is no trigger detector.

Two things to keep in mind: the voltages (410 V mesh, 710 V drift) are set by hand and are not recorded by the DAQ, so we note them in a logbook; and the gas (Ar/isobutane 95/5) is not yet confirmed.

2. A word about the threshold knob

The threshold of a chip is a number (sdt, in “DAC units”, just a counting scale). Each chip rests at a slightly different level, its baseline: a threshold below the baseline makes the chip fire all the time. So one common number for all chips is a trap.

Estimated baselines from the July beam data. With one common threshold of 200, the chips above the band would fire all the time.

That is why the scan is written as d = threshold − baseline for each chip: d = 50 is a comfortable margin, d = 16 is close to the noise. These baselines are only estimates from July. Remember this: it turns out to matter.

3. Step by step

Each step below says what we did, what we saw, what it told us, and why the next step follows. Nothing is skipped.

1A first test with the July settings done
What we did
We ran the July chip settings for 2 minutes with the chambers at voltage.
What we saw
1.5 GB of data in 2 minutes: about 1.6 million hits per second, almost all from 3 chips (VMM 4, 9 and later 7).
What it tells us
Something fires non-stop on those chips. A scan with this flood would fill the disk and hide the other chips.
So the next step is
find which channels fire, and try to switch them off (mask them).
2Which channels? Try masking done
What we did
We counted hits per channel, switched off the hottest ones, and tried again.
What we saw
A single channel gave nearly everything (VMM 4 ch 49: 99 % of that chip). Once masked, the burst jumped to other channels (it “walks”). Masking whole chips VMM 4 and 9 brought the data from 635 to 5 MB per minute.
What it tells us
Masking channel by channel does not work for these chips, so we leave VMM 4 and 9 out of the scan (they give no curve). The others are quiet enough.
So the next step is
scan the threshold on all the other chips.

Real counts. Switching off the hot channels did not help: the burst moved to channels 20 and 21 on VMM 9, and 36 and 38 on VMM 4.

3Noise scan with the chamber voltage ON done
What we did
Ten threshold steps, d = 50 down to 16, 2 minutes each. The chip settings are the same except the threshold.
What we saw
Almost nothing at d = 50. As d falls, chips switch on one after the other, and then climb very fast. VMM 15 starts at d = 34, VMM 10 only at d = 18.
What it tells us
The chips do not start to be noisy at the same distance above the baselines we assumed (a spread of 16 steps), so our baselines are not right. But is the spread a property of the electronics or of the chambers?
So the next step is
repeat the same scan with the voltage off.

Real hit rates. Each row is a chip, each column a threshold step (quiet on the left). The orange outline is the first step above 1 kHz. Hover a cell for the number.

4. Why switch the voltage off?

With the voltage on, a hit can come from the electronics or from the detector (a muon, a discharge, small pad noise). With the voltage off, the gas gives no signal, so every hit must come from the electronics. Doing the same scan twice separates the two.

The idea of the two scans. Same chips, same settings, same cables; only the chamber voltage changes.

4The same scan with the voltage OFF done
What we did
You switched all six high-voltage channels off by hand. We ran the same ten steps, and then four more steps with nothing masked at all, to see the burst chips.
What we saw
At d = 50 every chip is at zero. Chips start to fire in nearly the same order as before, usually 0 to 6 steps later (VMM 5 is the biggest exception). VMM 4, 7 and 9 still burst. Some channels are still noisy.
What it tells us
The spread between chips and the bursts are properties of the electronics (chip, hybrid or cable). The small background at d = 50 and most noisy channels need the detector.
So the next step is
understand a strange feature we saw: some chips’ rate falls when we lower the threshold further.

Real hit rates with the voltage off, same layout as before. VMM 4 and 9 are masked as in the first scan.

The first step above 1 kHz for each chip, voltage on (blue dot) and off (orange ring). Where they sit together, the ring surrounds the dot. The order of the chips is almost the same, and the spread of about 16 steps is still there with the voltage off.

Data per minute, voltage on and off. At moderate thresholds the voltage adds a lot of hits; at the lowest thresholds the electronics dominate and the two get closer.

The three burst chips with nothing masked and the voltage off. They burst all the same, so the cause is not the pads: it is the chip, the hybrid or the cable.

Channel by channel: for every channel that fired with the voltage on, did it still fire with the voltage off? Most disappear (blue). VMM 12 and VMM 15 keep many channels (orange): the whole chip is noisy by itself.

5. The strange curves, explained

In the voltage-on scan, some chips got quieter at the lowest thresholds (for example VMM 5: 416 kHz at d = 20, then 0.3 kHz at d = 18). Lowering a threshold should never give fewer hits… unless we look at one channel at a time.

A channel records a hit each time its (noisy) baseline crosses the threshold going up. Far above, the noise rarely reaches it. Far below, the channel is always above the threshold, so there are no new crossings. The most crossings happen when the threshold sits at the channel’s own baseline.

Real data: single channels follow that bell shape. VMM 7 channel 23 peaks at d = 34 and VMM 5 channel 46 at d = 20, with or without voltage. The place of the peak is the baseline of that channel.

5Look at single channels done
What we did
We followed individual channels across the threshold steps.
What we saw
Each channel has a bell-shaped curve with a peak. Different channels of the same chip peak at different steps (for VMM 7: channel 23 peaks at step 34, while channel 10 is still rising at step 16).
What it tells us
Inside one chip the channels have different baselines. One high channel can make the whole chip look noisy, and a single threshold per chip cannot suit all channels. This is a probable explanation; we have not yet measured the baselines directly.
So the next step is
measure the baseline of every channel directly (next section).

6. Measuring the baselines directly

Up to here the baselines of the channels were only guesses: estimates from the July beam data, and the positions of the noise peaks. Can we measure them? Yes, with a setting of the chip called sng = 1 (“neighbour read-out”). When one channel fires, its two neighbours are read out too, even though they are below threshold. A neighbour that is read out without any pulse reports the level it rests at: its baseline.

A firing channel (orange) makes its two neighbours (blue) report their ADC value. The hit word carries a flag that tells the two cases apart, so the data contain both the hits and the baselines.

The catch: with noise as the only trigger, only the neighbours of channels that fire are covered. And a channel fires most at its own bell-curve peak (section 5), so each threshold step makes different channels fire. A short ladder of thresholds therefore adds up to many channels:

Each step lets other channels fire, and their neighbours get measured. The cartoon uses 16 channels and three steps.

6Neighbour read-out with the voltage OFF done
What we did
We switched on sng = 1 on every chip and took 2 minutes at five settings: one first test, and then four thresholds per chip, 0, 3, 6 and 9 steps lower than that chip’s noise onset from step 4. VMM 4 and 9 stay masked. The voltage stayed off, so the baselines are measured without any detector effect.
What we saw
Between 64 and 67 % of the hits on every chip are neighbour read-outs, as expected. After adding the five captures, 518 of the 1024 channels on the 16 working chips had a baseline (at least 50 read-outs); the count grew at every step (335, 337, 352, 426, 518). The median baseline is 54 ADC; chip medians range from 47.5 to 60.5; only 8 of the 518 channels are at or below 38.
What it tells us
The single value used so far for every channel (38) is wrong for almost all of them, and the baselines differ between chips and, by a few ADC counts, inside each chip. About half of the channels still have no measurement: only a few seconds of each 2-minute capture were analysed, and more time, or cosmic muons with the voltage on (they hit all channels evenly), would add more.
So the next step is
put the baselines in threshold units (millivolts), which needs one more measurement (step 7).

Real data: every cell is one channel. Hatched cells have no measurement yet. Hover a cell for the value and the number of read-outs. VMM 6 and VMM 7 are mostly hatched: they have the fewest measurements.

The same, chip by chip: the bar runs from the lowest to the highest channel, the dot is the chip median. Every chip sits well to the right of the old value of 38. VMM 16 has one channel far outside the plot (ADC 704, channel 33), not understood yet.

How the coverage grew as captures were added (cumulative): the first test, then the four steps. The last steps, at the lowest thresholds, still added many channels, so the ladder works.

Does it fit the bell curves of section 5?

If a channel fires most when the threshold sits at its baseline, then a channel with a higher baseline should peak at a higher threshold step. We can now test this. The voltage-off scan does not reach its peaks (the rates still rise at the last step), so we used the peak positions from the voltage-on scan and the baselines measured with the voltage off (the baseline is an electronics property).

Each dot is a channel with both a measured baseline and a clear noise peak (32 channels here, peaks with at least 10 hits). The trend is positive, as predicted, but weak. With stricter or looser requirements on the peak the rank correlation is +0.56 (10 channels, at least 30 hits), +0.30 (32 channels, at least 10 hits), +0.35 (66 channels, at least 5 hits).

So the data agree with the explanation, but they do not prove it: there are few channels, the threshold steps are coarse (3 to 10 units) and the peaks have few hits. A scan with finer steps on the same chips would settle it.

The chain we are building. We set the threshold in DAC steps and see the baseline in ADC counts. To correct channel by channel we also need the level in millivolts, which the chip can report through its monitor.

7. What we know, and what we do not

We know

  • With no voltage, the electronics are silent at d = 50.
  • The chip-to-chip spread of the noise onset (about 16 steps) is an electronics effect.
  • VMM 4, 7 and 9 have noisy channels that need no voltage.
  • VMM 12 and VMM 15 are noisy over many channels without voltage.
  • A single channel’s noise rate has a bell-shaped curve with a peak.
  • With neighbour read-out we can measure a channel’s baseline directly. The value used so far (38) is wrong for nearly all channels; the chips differ (medians 47.5 to 60.5).
  • The measured baselines point the same way as the peak positions, but only weakly.

We do not know yet

  • The efficiency: this was the noise side only.
  • The baseline of the other half of the channels (still to be covered), and whether the voltage changes the baselines (same measurement with the voltage on).
  • The link between ADC counts and millivolts, channel by channel.
  • Why VMM 16 channel 33 reads an ADC of 704.
  • Why VMM 4 and 9 burst, and whether the cable, the hybrid or the new firmware is to blame.
  • Whether the lab noise looks like the beam-hall noise (about 100 times higher in the beam hall).
  • The gas, and the measured voltages (they are set by hand and not logged).

8. What comes next

Today each chip has one threshold. The next step gives each channel a small correction so that all channels start at the same level (cartoon, not measured).

What is needed
A way to call the library: either ask the developer to add a command, or write a small program of our own that calls it (without changing his software). We would test it on one channel of one hybrid first, then run all channels of all hybrids.
