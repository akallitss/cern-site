Measuring the VMM noise threshold with cosmic muons: what we did and what we saw

A picture book of the first lab threshold scan on the three-chamber cosmic stand: why, how, and the first noise results per chip.

Detector R&D 2026-10-22 VMM,threshold,noise,cosmics,P2,lab
In one minute.
  • The question: how low can we set the threshold of each VMM chip before electronic noise takes over? The beam test could only guess the answer. Here, with cosmic muons, we can measure it.
  • What we did, in order: (1) a first test, which showed a flood of data from a few chips; (2) a noise scan with the chamber voltage on; (3) the same scan with the voltage off, to separate the detector from the electronics; (4) a closer look at single channels; (5) a direct measurement of the baseline of each channel, using a chip setting where a firing channel makes its neighbours report; (6) the same scans with the three chips we had masked (VMM 4, 7, 9) switched back on; (7) the first measurements with the high voltage on: a health check, and the neighbour read-out with cosmic muons, which gave a baseline for 97 % of the channels.
  • What we found: the chips do not start to be noisy at the same threshold, and this spread stays when the voltage is off, so it comes from the electronics, not the chambers. Three chips (VMM 4, 7, 9) have noisy channels that also stay with the voltage off; when we switch them back on they show two kinds of noise: an ordinary smooth onset like the other chips, plus bursts that jump up and down with each new configuration, even at very high thresholds. And each channel has a noise peak of its own, so the channels inside one chip differ too. The baselines we could measure directly (about half of the channels so far) are mostly 45 to 65 ADC counts, not the 38 used so far for every channel, and they differ from chip to chip. With the voltage on we now have a baseline for 97 % of the channels, the voltage does not change them, and VMM 4, 7 and 9 each have a large block of channels with baselines of 100 to several hundred, next to the channels that burst.
  • What this means for the next step: one threshold per chip is not enough. We need the baseline of every channel (half is done), in threshold units (millivolts, not yet done), and then to correct each channel. Then we repeat the scan and, only then, measure efficiency. The measurement with muon tracks (reference run and threshold scan, high voltage on) is running now, with a global threshold for each chip read from the noise scans; the reference run (8 hours) and the threshold scan are done and show a steady muon rate of about 21 per minute for the stack, but also bursts that carry a quarter of the hits and have to be cut. An interleaved 24-hour run at four thresholds is taking the statistics for the efficiency.
  • Update, Sunday 25 October evening (step 10c): the tag-and-probe analysis, with the beam-test pad map, shows that the “muon-like” events are not muon tracks: their hits do not line up in a straight line through the three chambers. Counting the events that do form a straight line, minus what chance gives, leaves about 16 per hour at the safe setting, where muons should give hundreds to thousands. Below the safe setting the extra “tracks” are a fixed pattern of a few hot channels. The pad map itself is fine (no other wiring does better, no chamber is flipped). So the stack sees almost no cosmic muons above the noise at these settings, and the best threshold cannot be chosen from efficiency yet. The long run was stopped at 21:34 (runs 96–111). Next: check the high voltage and the gas against the beam test, and a source test.

0. The idea in one picture

A VMM channel records a hit when the pulse it sees goes above a threshold. If the threshold is high, small muon pulses are missed (low efficiency). If it is low, electronic noise also goes above it (many false hits). Move the slider to feel the trade-off:

Muon signals seen (efficiency)
Noise hits per second, relative

Cartoon with invented shapes: the point is the trade-off, not the numbers.

In the SPS beam data the VMM efficiency was about 85 % against 95.6 % for the DREAM readout of the same chambers, and the analysis said the VMM threshold was probably too high. That was a prediction: the beam gave no time to vary the threshold. In the lab we can, because cosmic muons arrive at a steady, gentle rate and we can wait.

1. The set-up

Three chambers stacked like at the beam test. Nine hybrids with two VMM chips each read them out (VMM 2 to 19). The chips decide for themselves when to record a hit; there is no trigger detector.

Two things to keep in mind: the voltages (410 V mesh, 710 V drift) are set by hand and are not recorded by the DAQ, so we note them in a logbook; and the gas (Ar/isobutane 95/5) is not yet confirmed.

2. A word about the threshold knob

The threshold of a chip is a number (sdt, in “DAC units”, just a counting scale). Each chip rests at a slightly different level, its baseline: a threshold below the baseline makes the chip fire all the time. So one common number for all chips is a trap.

Estimated baselines from the July beam data. With one common threshold of 200, the chips above the band would fire all the time.

That is why the scan is written as d = threshold − baseline for each chip: d = 50 is a comfortable margin, d = 16 is close to the noise. These baselines are only estimates from July. Remember this: it turns out to matter.

3. Step by step

Each step below says what we did, what we saw, what it told us, and why the next step follows. Nothing is skipped.

1A first test with the July settings done
What we did
We ran the July chip settings for 2 minutes with the chambers at voltage.
What we saw
1.5 GB of data in 2 minutes: about 1.6 million hits per second, almost all from 3 chips (VMM 4, 9 and later 7).
What it tells us
Something fires non-stop on those chips. A scan with this flood would fill the disk and hide the other chips.
So the next step is
find which channels fire, and try to switch them off (mask them).
2Which channels? Try masking done
What we did
We counted hits per channel, switched off the hottest ones, and tried again.
What we saw
A single channel gave nearly everything (VMM 4 ch 49: 99 % of that chip). Once masked, the burst jumped to other channels (it “walks”). Masking whole chips VMM 4 and 9 brought the data from 635 to 5 MB per minute.
What it tells us
Masking channel by channel does not work for these chips, so we leave VMM 4 and 9 out of the scan (they give no curve). The others are quiet enough.
So the next step is
scan the threshold on all the other chips.

Real counts. Switching off the hot channels did not help: the burst moved to channels 20 and 21 on VMM 9, and 36 and 38 on VMM 4.

3Noise scan with the chamber voltage ON done
What we did
Ten threshold steps, d = 50 down to 16, 2 minutes each. The chip settings are the same except the threshold.
What we saw
Almost nothing at d = 50. As d falls, chips switch on one after the other, and then climb very fast. VMM 15 starts at d = 34, VMM 10 only at d = 18.
What it tells us
The chips do not start to be noisy at the same distance above the baselines we assumed (a spread of 16 steps), so our baselines are not right. But is the spread a property of the electronics or of the chambers?
So the next step is
repeat the same scan with the voltage off.

4. Why switch the voltage off?

With the voltage on, a hit can come from the electronics or from the detector (a muon, a discharge, small pad noise). With the voltage off, the gas gives no signal, so every hit must come from the electronics. Doing the same scan twice separates the two.

The idea of the two scans. Same chips, same settings, same cables; only the chamber voltage changes.

4The same scan with the voltage OFF done
What we did
You switched all six high-voltage channels off by hand. We ran the same ten steps, and then four more steps with nothing masked at all, to see the burst chips.
What we saw
At d = 50 every chip is at zero. Chips start to fire in nearly the same order as before, usually 0 to 6 steps later (VMM 5 is the biggest exception). VMM 4, 7 and 9 still burst. Some channels are still noisy.
What it tells us
The spread between chips and the bursts are properties of the electronics (chip, hybrid or cable). The small background at d = 50 and most noisy channels need the detector.
So the next step is
understand a strange feature we saw: some chips’ rate falls when we lower the threshold further.

5. The strange curves, explained

In the voltage-on scan, some chips got quieter at the lowest thresholds (for example VMM 5: 416 kHz at d = 20, then 0.3 kHz at d = 18). Lowering a threshold should never give fewer hits… unless we look at one channel at a time.

A channel records a hit each time its (noisy) baseline crosses the threshold going up. Far above, the noise rarely reaches it. Far below, the channel is always above the threshold, so there are no new crossings. The most crossings happen when the threshold sits at the channel’s own baseline.

Real data: single channels follow that bell shape. VMM 7 channel 23 peaks at d = 34 and VMM 5 channel 46 at d = 20, with or without voltage. The place of the peak tells where the threshold meets that channel’s level.

5Look at single channels done
What we did
We followed individual channels across the threshold steps.
What we saw
Each channel has a bell-shaped curve with a peak. Different channels of the same chip peak at different steps (for VMM 7: channel 23 peaks at step 34, while channel 10 is still rising at step 16).
What it tells us
Inside one chip the channels peak at different steps, so the level that the threshold has to clear differs from channel to channel. One high channel can make the whole chip look noisy, and a single threshold per chip cannot suit all channels. Our picture is that the peak sits where the threshold meets the channel’s own level; the next section tests this.
So the next step is
measure the baseline of every channel directly (next section).

6. Measuring the baselines directly

Up to here the baselines of the channels were only guesses: estimates from the July beam data, and the positions of the noise peaks. Can we measure them? Yes, with a setting of the chip called sng = 1 (“neighbour read-out”). When one channel fires, its two neighbours are read out too, even though they are below threshold. A neighbour that is read out without any pulse reports the level it rests at: its baseline.

A firing channel (orange) makes its two neighbours (blue) report their ADC value. The hit word carries a flag that tells the two cases apart, so the data contain both the hits and the baselines.

The catch: with noise as the only trigger, only the neighbours of channels that fire are covered. And a channel fires most at its own bell-curve peak (section 5), so each threshold step makes different channels fire. A short ladder of thresholds therefore adds up to many channels:

Each step lets other channels fire, and their neighbours get measured. The cartoon uses 16 channels and three steps.

6Neighbour read-out with the voltage OFF done
What we did
We switched on sng = 1 on every chip and took 2 minutes at five settings: one first test, and then four thresholds per chip, 0, 3, 6 and 9 steps lower than that chip’s noise onset from step 4. VMM 4 and 9 stay masked. The voltage stayed off, so the baselines are measured without any detector effect.
What we saw
Between 64 and 67 % of the hits on every chip are neighbour read-outs, as expected. After adding the five captures, 627 of the 1024 channels on the 16 working chips had a baseline (at least 50 read-outs); the count after each capture was 410, 410, 428, 528, 627, and the two lowest thresholds added the most. The whole of each 2-minute capture was used (599 s in total). The median baseline is 54 ADC; chip medians range from 48 to 61; only 10 of the 627 channels are at or below 38.
What it tells us
The single value used so far for every channel (38) is wrong for almost all of them, and the baselines differ between chips and, by a few ADC counts, inside each chip. All the data taken have now been used, so more coverage needs new data: more steps, longer runs, or cosmic muons with the voltage on (they hit all channels evenly).

Does the baseline predict where a channel’s noise peaks?

If a channel fires most when the threshold sits at its baseline, then a channel with a higher measured baseline should peak at a higher threshold step. We can now test this. The voltage-off scan does not reach its peaks (the rates still rise at the last step), so we used the peak positions from the voltage-on scan and the baselines measured with the voltage off (the baseline is an electronics property).

Each dot is a channel with both a measured baseline and a clear noise peak (39 channels here, peaks with at least 10 hits). A line through the dots is almost flat. The rank correlation is +0.48 (13 channels, at least 30 hits), +0.33 (39 channels, at least 10 hits), +0.17 (82 channels, at least 5 hits); it gets smaller as more channels are included, and the ordinary correlation is small (+0.04 to +0.21). Channels above 100 ADC (the three outliers) are left out of the plot and of these numbers.

So the answer is no, at least not clearly. An earlier look with fewer channels showed a weak positive trend; with the whole data it largely disappears. Three reasons are possible, and none is tested yet: (1) the ADC baseline is not the same thing as the level the threshold acts on, because each channel’s threshold comparator has its own small offset; (2) the threshold steps are coarse (3 to 10 units) and many peaks have few hits; (3) the peaks come from the voltage-on scan and the baselines from the voltage-off scan. Reason (1) is exactly why the chip monitor measurement of step 12 matters: it measures the pedestal and the threshold of each channel, so it gives the missing link.

The chain we are building. We set the threshold in DAC steps and see the baseline in ADC counts. To correct channel by channel we also need the level in millivolts, which the chip can report through its monitor.

7The three burst chips, unmasked done
What we did
We repeated the voltage-off scan with VMM 4, 7 and 9 switched back on, at 13 thresholds with the normal read-out (d = 200 down to 16) and 9 with neighbour read-out (d = 100 down to 14), the other chips at d = 50. We also read the temperature of all chips (31 to 38 °C, nothing unusual).
What we saw
Two things. (a) A smooth noise onset like the other chips: VMM 4 passes 1 kHz at d = 22 (hottest channels 37 and 35) and VMM 9 at d = 27 (channel 16); VMM 7 shows none down to d = 16. (b) Bursts of up to millions of hits per second that do not follow the threshold: VMM 9 gives 2.7 MHz at d = 200, far above any baseline, and VMM 4 gives 0 at d = 60 but 1.8 MHz at d = 40. The hot channel changes with each configuration (VMM 4: channel 45, 39, 38/36, then 37/35). Within one run it does not change, and its pulse height is almost constant.
What it tells us
The chips can be placed on the same threshold scale as the rest (the smooth part), and the bursts are not ordinary noise: they look like a state a channel gets stuck in when the chip is configured. We have not yet tested whether applying the same configuration twice gives the same state.
So the next step is
repeat a few configurations several times (a short run, about 15 minutes, with the voltage off) to see whether the burst state is repeatable.

8. First measurements with the voltage on

The high voltage was switched on after the runs above (we declare it in the run files; the voltages themselves are not read by the DAQ and are still to be written in the logbook). Three things were done with it on: a health check of the chambers, the neighbour read-out measurement again but now with cosmic muons, and the noise ladder of VMM 4, 7 and 9 again.

8a. Is every chamber alive?

We took 10 minutes at the “safe” setting of each chip (8 steps above the noise onset we measured with the voltage on) and looked at the pulse heights of the hits.

Real data, 2 minutes of the 10-minute run. Every chip has hits and a broad spectrum. VMM 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7 of chamber det3 reach the ADC limit; the other two chambers stay between about 500 and 850.

No chamber looks like the July failure of det3 (a collapsed gain). The det3 chips reaching the ADC limit is unexplained: it could be a higher gain than the other chambers or discharges, and the spectrum shape with more statistics will tell. And 6 to 100 hits per second and chip is well above the cosmic rate we expect (about 4 per second), so at this setting most hits are noise or background, and muon tracks will be picked out by requiring a coincidence of the three chambers.

8b. A baseline for (nearly) every channel

With the voltage off only channels that fire by themselves were covered, about 40 % of the channels were still missing. With the voltage on, cosmic muons and chamber activity make every pad fire sometimes, so every channel becomes the neighbour of a firing one. We ran the neighbour read-out at five thresholds (10 minutes each, 50 minutes in total).

Channels with a measured baseline: 1123 of 1152 (97 %) with the voltage on, against 667 with the voltage off. 14 of the 18 chips are complete. The missing ones are mostly the masked channels of the three burst chips.

Does the voltage change the baseline? We compared, for the 15 normal chips, the 609 channels measured both ways:

The median difference is +1.0 ADC count (typical spread 1), 70 % of the channels agree within 3 counts and 82 % within 5. So the voltage does not move the baselines of these chips, and the voltage-off measurement was fine for them.

Over all 1123 channels the median baseline is 51 ADC, and only 85 channels (8 %) are at or below the 38 we had assumed for all of them.

8c. VMM 4, 7 and 9 have a block of channels with a high baseline

With complete coverage the three burst chips look different from every other chip. Among the 15 normal chips just one channel out of about 960 has a baseline above 80 ADC (VMM 16 channel 33, which reads 704). On VMM 4, 7 and 9 a large block of channels does:

10. First look at the muon events

The reference run is already taking data with the thresholds above. Can we find the muons in it? We took the first 30 minutes (the voltage on, the safe setting, the 23 hot channels of VMM 4, 7 and 9 masked) and grouped the hits that happen at the same moment.

The three kinds of event we expect to see in the three chambers.

Real data. 1,828,316 hits in 30 minutes, 1,074,258 groups. More than half of the groups are a single, isolated hit; 1,429 groups have more than 36 hits and carry 26 % of all hits.

Yes, the muons are there: 1,140 groups have hits in all three chambers, and 645 of them are small (at most 12 hits per chamber, typically exactly one per chamber), which is what a muon looks like. That is about 19 per minute for the whole stack. The time difference between the chambers in these events is centred on zero (the first hit of each chamber, median 0 to −7 ns), so there is no big constant timing offset between the hybrids, and the spread is about 40 ns, because the fine timing is not calibrated yet.

Correction (25 October, step 10c). “Yes, the muons are there” was too quick. The later analysis put each hit at its pad position and asked whether the three hits form a straight line: almost none of these events do, beyond what chance gives. Most of the small three-chamber events are noise that fires the chambers at the same moment. The text above is kept as it was written.

The complications we have to deal with (this is what makes the correlation between the chambers harder than it looks):

  • Bursts. A quarter of all hits sit in a few thousand groups with dozens to hundreds of hits across many chips and chambers at the same moment. If we just ask for hits in all three chambers within 100 ns, these bursts give millions of false pairs, against a few thousand real ones. They must be cut by size before anything else. We do not know what they are (discharges in the chambers, or pick-up in the electronics); the repeat test with the voltage off would tell us.
  • Noise. Half of all hits are isolated singles. They cost nothing in a three-chamber coincidence, but they fake a probe hit: the efficiency must be corrected for the chance that a noise hit lands next to the muon’s track.
  • Few tracks. About 19 per minute for the whole stack, fewer than the 40 per chip we hoped for (not yet corrected for the masked channels, the pad size or the angles). An hour gives about 1100 tracks for the stack, so an efficiency per chip is limited to a few per cent per hour. We need hours of data per setting.
  • Timing. The timing is not calibrated (about 40 ns spread), so the coincidence window must be about ±100 ns. This is fine at these rates.
  • Geometry. We still have to map the pads of each chip to positions, check the orientation of each chamber, and use the beam-test distances. Cosmic muons arrive at wide angles, so the line between two chambers must allow a wide search box in the third.
  • The three burst chips. Their masked channels leave holes in the efficiency map, and their bursts must be rejected by the size cut as well.
  • det3. Its pulse heights reach the ADC limit (step 8). That is a question of gain or discharges, and it matters for how we define a hit.

10b. The weekend runs: what the muon data look like over a day

Since the first look the stack took 8 hours at the “safe” setting (16 runs of 30 minutes, all complete) and then four one-hour points at other thresholds. The same grouping of hits was applied to every run.

Real data. Left: over the 8 hours the muon-like rate stays at 20.8 ± 1.1 per minute for the stack, with no drift; the total hit rate, however, rises by about half (914 to 1430 per second) and the large bursts go from 0.7 to 1.2 per second, for a reason we do not know. Right: the number of muon-like events per minute at five settings.

Reading the right-hand panel: from 16 steps above the safe setting down to 4 steps below it, the yield rises from 13 to 22 per minute (steps of +22 %, +27 %, +6 %), and the share of the hits that sit in large bursts falls from 27 % to 13 % because more ordinary hits join. At 8 steps below the safe setting the number jumps to 469 per minute: the chips are now so noisy that chance coincidences between the three chambers look like muons. So the useful range ends somewhere between 4 and 8 steps below the safe setting.

This does not tell us the best threshold. The yield counts real muons and chance coincidences together. The flattening between 0 and −4 hints that the efficiency is levelling off, but only the tag-and-probe analysis, with the noise correction, can show it. What the runs do show is that the muon rate is steady, the time offsets between the chambers are stable (−11 to +1 ns, spread 35 to 48 ns in every run), and that the interesting settings are the ones from 8 steps above the safe setting down to about 6 steps below it.

10c. Are these really muons? The tag-and-probe analysis

The idea. The two outer chambers “tag” a muon; the middle chamber is the “probe”: did it see the muon too? The fraction of yes answers is the efficiency. This is the same method as at the beam test, with the outer chambers playing the role of the reference detectors. It only works if the tags really are muons.

First try, without positions. We just asked: when det3 and det1 both fire, does det2 fire at the same moment?

settingdet2 fired when det3 and det1 fired
safe (16 repeats, runs 76–91)0.52–0.58
safe + 8 (runs 93, 96)0.50, 0.46
safe + 16 (run 92)0.49
safe − 4 (run 94)0.36
safe − 8 (run 95, a quarter of the files)0.04

This looks like an efficiency of about 55 % at the safe setting that falls at lower thresholds. It is not one. At lower thresholds the outer chambers fire together on noise much more often, so the “tags” are mostly fake and the fraction drops; and a det2 hit anywhere in the chamber counts, not only where the muon went.

Second try, with positions. We put every hit on its pad with the beam-test map and asked whether the det2 hit is where the line through the outer hits says it should be.

Lying flat, 31 cm apart: a straight line crosses the middle chamber half way between its crossings of the outer two, whatever its angle. This test needs no other detector.

Only 3.6 % of the tags had a det2 hit at the right place, although 63 % had a det2 hit somewhere at the right time. Either the map is wrong for this set-up (new Samtec cables, chambers turned flat), or the events are not muons. We checked both.

Is the map wrong? We followed the method used on the cosmic bench: try every reasonable way the 64 channels of each chip could be wired to the pads (40 per connector: in order, reversed, pairs swapped, the two halves swapped, odd and even pins split), and keep the one that makes the most events straight. To be fair, we fitted on half of the events and tested on the other half.

Real data, runs 76–91. The beam-test map does best on events it has never seen. A different wiring can look better on the events it was tuned on, but it does worse on new events: it learned the noise. We also scanned rotations and mirror images between the chambers: no chamber is flipped.

One detail is useful for the future: at the beam test two connector halves of P2_IN needed a correction because of the Hitachi cables. With the Samtec cables they follow the plain order.

So are the events muons? We counted, for every run, the groups that contain a straight triplet (one hit per chamber, middle hit within 30 mm of the midpoint), and subtracted what chance gives. Chance is measured by moving each middle-chamber hit to another channel of the same chip: this keeps the noise that is shared between chips, and only real straight lines survive.

Real data, runs 76–108, all settings of the threshold scan and of the long run. At the safe setting about 16 per hour, from 10.6 hours. For comparison, a rough estimate: the stack covers about 0.05 m² per chamber, so muons crossing all three should be of order one per second, that is a few thousand per hour times the efficiency of each chamber to the third power (about 400 per hour at 50 % per chamber).

11. What we know, and what we do not

We know

  • With no voltage, the electronics are silent at d = 50.
  • The chip-to-chip spread of the noise onset (about 16 steps) is an electronics effect.
  • VMM 4, 7 and 9 have noisy channels that need no voltage. Unmasked, they also show an ordinary smooth noise onset (VMM 4 at d = 22, VMM 9 at d = 27) plus bursts whose size and channel change with each configuration.
  • VMM 12 and VMM 15 are noisy over many channels without voltage.
  • A single channel’s noise rate has a bell-shaped curve with a peak.
  • With neighbour read-out we can measure a channel’s baseline directly. The value used so far (38) is wrong for nearly all channels; the chips differ (medians 48 to 61).
  • The measured ADC baselines do not predict where a channel’s noise peaks (correlation between 0 and 0.4).
  • With the voltage on, 97 % of the channels have a baseline, and the voltage does not change them on the 15 normal chips.
  • VMM 4, 7 and 9 each have a block of channels with a high baseline (also with the voltage off); the bursts are at its edge and, at high thresholds, are the same with the voltage on and off.
  • No chamber is dead with the voltage on.
  • A global threshold for 13 of the 18 chips can be read from the noise scans (onset + 8 steps); the five others are special.
  • Muon-like events are in the data (about 21 per minute for the stack, steady over 8 hours), with no large timing offset between the chambers, and the usable range of thresholds ends between 4 and 8 steps below the safe setting, where noise coincidences take over. Corrected on 25 October: these are mostly shared noise, not muons (next item).
  • Only about 16 per hour of the three-chamber events are straight tracks beyond chance, at every setting from 16 steps above to 4 below the safe one; the extra “tracks” at lower thresholds are a fixed pattern of hot channels.
  • The beam-test channel-to-pad map is the best one for this set-up; no chamber is flipped or mirrored; the two P2_IN corrections of the Hitachi cables are not needed with the Samtec cables.

We do not know yet

  • The efficiency: this was the noise side and a first look at the muon events.
  • Which threshold gives the best efficiency (the yield rises down to 4 steps below the safe setting, but it mixes muons and chance coincidences), and what the large bursts (a quarter of all hits) are.
  • Why the chambers show almost no muon signal here, when they gave 67–85 % at the beam test: high voltage, gas, cables?
  • Why the total hit rate rose by half over the 8 hours of the reference run while the muon rate did not.
  • Why the three burst chips have that block of channels, and whether it is the chip, the hybrid or the connection.
  • The link between ADC counts and millivolts, channel by channel.
  • Whether the ADC baseline and the level the threshold acts on are the same thing, and why three channels read very high values (VMM 16 ch 33: 704, VMM 6 ch 14: 186, VMM 14 ch 34: 125).
  • Whether applying the very same configuration twice on the same day gives the same burst (the Monday repeat test), and what the det3 pulse heights that reach the ADC limit mean.
  • Whether the lab noise looks like the beam-hall noise (about 100 times higher in the beam hall).
  • What the voltage actually is: it is set by hand and not logged.
