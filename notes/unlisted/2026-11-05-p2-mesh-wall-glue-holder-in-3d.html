What you are looking at

The board is the bottom straight edge between x = 302 and 378 mm, as measured from the V1 films and the Apr26 PCB: insulation frame (blue) out to the board edge, the 2.66 mm Dynamask rim wall (green), the 0.57 mm M1 line under its inner border, copper pads, and the 0.8 mm pillars on their real 4 mm lattice. The two Ø3.5 mm screw holes at x = 318.3 and 354.8 mm (y = −7.5) are the ones the tools are pinned to. The PCB core is 0.2 mm, so the board is shown on a flat table.

A · sliding shoe, your way

  • Two runners on the wall's borders (0.35 mm wide, PTFE-faced), one on each edge of the wall. The inner runner is the dam: glue cannot pass under it towards the pillars.
  • The glue cavity between them is fed by the syringe. At the back, a lip 1.0 mm wide sits at the laid-height above the wall and leaves a line of that height.
  • Two overflow channels, one along the inside of each runner, between runner and lip. Glue pushed sideways goes up into them instead of under the runners. They collect what overflows, so the line that stays on the wall is the one the lip sets. The top of the wall is flat, so the shoe, not the wall, does the containing: the runners have to be kept on the wall's borders by the user.
  • A fence arm rides against a printed guide bar pinned in two screw holes, which keeps the runners on the 2.66 mm wall. A straight bar for each straight side, an arc bar for each arc.
  • Print the body. Make the runners and lip from shim or PTFE strip glued in: 0.35 mm features are below what FDM holds.

The overflow channels (magenta) are under the shoe, so they are hidden from above. Press end-on, or tick cut through the tool, to see them in section.

B · film stencil

  • A 50–100 µm PET or Kapton film with a 0.7 mm slot over the middle of the wall, pinned in the same screw holes. It rests on the wall and on the outer pillar tops.
  • The squeegee fills the slot. The film rests on the pillar tops, which stand 18 µm (the copper) above the wall, so lifting it leaves a line as tall as the film plus 18 µm.

C · one shoe, guided by the board outline

The shoe is the same as in A: two runners, the glue cavity, the lip and the two overflow channels, as in A. The difference is how it is kept on the wall. There is no set of pinned bars: the shoe is slid all the way round in one pass, and a fence arm rides against the outline of the board.

  • The board edge alone is too thin to steer on. The stack-up is 0.2 mm FR4 + 2 × 18 µm copper, so the edge is 0.24 mm tall. The board therefore lies in a nest plate: a plate cut to the Edge_Cuts outline, about 2 mm thicker than the board, located with pins in two or three screw holes (drilled holes are more accurate than the routed edge). Its rim is the fence for the whole perimeter, in one piece.
  • A short shoe, 10 mm instead of 16 mm, so it follows the arcs. The fence arm has two PTFE buttons against the rim; you push the shoe forward and slightly outwards.
  • The arm reach changes with the side (table below): 12 mm for the bottom and the 60° side, 17 mm for the inner arc, 52 mm for the outer arc. Make it three swappable arms, or one arm with a slot and a screw.

  • About 1580 mm of the 1684 mm can follow the nest rim: the bottom, the 60° side and the inner arc with the 12/17 mm arms, and the outer arc with the 52 mm arm. The connectors are not soldered when the mesh is glued, so the long arm passes over the bare connector pads. Keep it clear of them (no contact) so the pads stay clean, and make it stiff (aluminium rather than print): over 52 mm a small flex already uses up the 0.3 mm margin.
  • The ~100 mm at the two ends of the outer arc have nothing parallel to follow: guide by hand there, or add two short corner pieces to the nest.
  • The routed edge has a break-out tab every 75 mm (about 3 mm wide, up to 0.5 mm proud). Sand the stubs flush so the board sits against the nest.
  • The margin is small: a runner can wander about 0.3 mm before it leaves the wall, and a routed edge is typically good to ±0.1–0.2 mm. That is why the nest is pinned in the screw holes and not just pushed against the board edge.

This C is new. The "C · fill the groove level" in the options note was a different idea (a flat blade on the rails); it is ruled out, because the top of the wall is flat.

The mesh step

In all three options the glue is spread by the weight of the mesh. The top of the wall is flat: there is no insulation step under it, so no rails and no groove (t₁ = 0). The mesh lands on the highest points, the pillars, which stand on 18 µm of copper and so are 18 µm above the wall. The glue fills that 18 µm gap and wets the mesh (~40 µm assumed), so the width it spreads to is volume ÷ (18 + 40 µm). That is the "after the mesh" number. It must stay inside the 2.66 mm wall, with some margin on both sides because nothing else stops it.

With the shoe (A or C) and a 100 µm laid line: 1.0 mm × 100 µm = 0.10 mm³ per mm, which spreads to about 1.7 mm, leaving about 0.47 mm to each border of the wall, and 0.17 mL for the whole detector. The width scales with the laid height (≈ 0.17 mm per 10 µm), and inversely with the mesh-wetting thickness, the one assumed number here.

Still open: the glue and its viscosity, and the mesh thickness after stretching (it sets the 40 µm above). Once these are known, the shoe, the nest or the stencil can be drawn for the workshop.
