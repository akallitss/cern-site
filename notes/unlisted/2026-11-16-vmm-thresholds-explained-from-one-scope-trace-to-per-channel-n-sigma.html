VMM thresholds explained: from one scope trace to per-channel n·σ

An animated walk-through for the talk. It starts from one pulse on an oscilloscope and scales up, one idea at a time, to the algorithm we use on the P2 cosmic stand: global threshold, per-channel trims, "raise instead of mask", and the next step, thresholds at baseline + n·σ with σ measured by the internal pulser. The last part shows the three tests that keep the σ honest: contamination, crosstalk and noise overestimation.

Overview

The question: how low can each channel go?

A VMM channel records a hit when its shaped signal crosses a threshold. Lower threshold → more of the small strip signals are kept (efficiency) but also more noise hits. Today one chip-wide threshold (sdt) is shared by 64 channels that do not have the same noise. The idea of this work: give every channel its own threshold, just above its own noise.

  1. One hit on a scope: signal, baseline, threshold, comparator.
  2. Noise alone: the Gaussian, and how fast a threshold at n·σ fires (Rice formula).
  3. Efficiency vs noise for a quiet and a noisy channel: why one threshold for both costs efficiency.
  4. 64 channels: global threshold + masks → equalised + "raise instead of mask" (done) → n·σ per channel (next).
  5. Measuring σ per channel with the internal pulser and neighbour read-out.
  6. Three tests: noisy-channel contamination, crosstalk, and is the pulser σ the real noise? (overestimation check).
  7. The full algorithm and its status.

Step 1 · one channel, one pulse

A single hit on the oscilloscope

This is what one VMM channel "sees" after its shaper (peaking time 200 ns, gain 4.5 mV/fC): a baseline that wiggles (electronic noise) and, when a particle passes, a shaped pulse. The comparator below fires while the trace is above the threshold. The rising edge is the hit (time stamp); the ADC then stores the pulse height.

Try it: lower the signal to ~12 mV → pulses are missed (inefficiency). Then lower the threshold to recover them → the baseline starts crossing on its own (noise hits, red). Every choice of threshold is a trade between these two.
Step 2 · noise alone

How often does pure noise cross a threshold?

Sample the baseline many times: the values form a Gaussian of width σ (left). Put the threshold at n·σ above the baseline. The rate of noise hits is not "the area of the tail"; it is the rate of up-crossings, given by Rice's formula:

R(n) = f₀ · exp(−n²/2)   with f₀ set by the shaper: 0.99 MHz (current noise) … 2.62 MHz (voltage noise) at 200 ns.

The exponential is brutal: every extra σ divides the noise rate by ~100 near n = 4–5. So the accepted noise rate fixes n: n = √(2·ln(f₀/R)). For R = 50 Hz per channel (the tuner's limit) n ≈ 4.5; for 5 Hz n ≈ 5. The same n means a different threshold in mV for channels with different σ.
Step 3 · two channels

A quiet and a noisy channel under one threshold

Blue: fraction of strip signals above the threshold (efficiency; a Landau-like spectrum of strip amplitudes, most-probable value adjustable). Orange/red: noise rate of a quiet channel (small σ, e.g. at the chip ends) and a noisy one (large σ, chip middle). Each channel has its own "noise edge" where its rate reaches the accepted limit.

A single threshold has to sit at the noisy channel's edge, or that channel floods (and in the past it was then masked). The quiet channel is then held far above its own noise: the green arrow is the efficiency it could recover with its own threshold.
Step 4 · one chip, 64 channels

Three ways to set 64 thresholds

Each column is one channel. The orange glow is its noise (taller = larger σ), the grey dash its noise edge for the accepted rate, the coloured tick its threshold (all in mV above the channel's own baseline). Switch strategy and watch the thresholds move.

  • A · global + masks (the wk2 scan files): one sdt; each channel's real distance is shifted by its comparator offset (measured spread 3.8 mV rms). Channels that end up too close to their noise are masked → lost entirely.
  • B · equalised + "raise instead of mask" (done, file trimmed_Km18_v1): trims bring every channel to the same mV distance (0.7 mV rms), then the tuner raises noisy channels by 3 trim steps per iteration. No masks, but every channel sits at the same distance, so the quiet ones keep a margin they do not need.
  • C · n·σ per channel (next): target = baseline + n·σc. The chip sdt goes to the highest target plus headroom; each trim lowers its channel to its own target. Quiet channels go lower than in B: that is the efficiency gain.
Tick "show sdt + trims" in C: a trim can only lower a channel (31 steps × ~1.1 mV ≈ 33 mV), so the chip level must sit above the noisiest channel's target and every other channel is pulled down from there. One very noisy channel can use up the trim range of the whole chip, which is why outliers are handled separately (raised by the tuner).

Lowering the whole chip further (−4 … −16 counts) ends with the tuner raising most channels back: with equal distances, the median channel's noise floor is ~3 mV below the K=−18 point. Equal distances is the limit of B; going further needs each channel's own σ → strategy C → we need σ per channel.

Step 5 · measuring σ

Internal pulser + neighbour read-out (method of I. Mandjavidze)

The FEC fires the chip's test pulser once per 4096-tick frame (10.85 kHz). Only channels with st 1 get the pulse: one channel in three (a "phase"). With neighbour read-out on (sng 1) the chip also reads the two neighbours of every pulsed channel, even below threshold. Those neighbour ADC values are pure baseline samples: their mean is the pedestal and their spread is σ. Three phases cover all 64 channels.

Test A — contamination by noisy channels. If the chip threshold is low, a noisy channel also fires on its own: its own above-threshold noise peaks land in its histogram (σ looks much bigger) and each of those hits reads out its neighbours too. Toggle the chip threshold to see it.

Measured (chips 2–3, 16 Nov): at K=−18 with the masks lifted, the pulsed channels of chip 3 got 1.7 million neighbour read-outs in 10 s that had nothing to do with the pulser; with the chip threshold +40 counts (~30 mV): 0. Every channel was then read exactly once per pulse (22 pulsed + 42 neighbours = 64). So: raise the chip threshold during the measurement. Result: pedestal 39–54 ADC, σ 2.5–5.8 ADC, low at the chip ends (2.5–3.7), high in the middle (4.5–5.8), exactly the per-channel information strategy B lacks.
Test B · crosstalk

The pulse leaks into the neighbours, and inflates σ

The neighbours are read at the moment their pulsed neighbour has a big pulse. A small fraction couples in: the pedestal moves up and σ grows with the pulse size. Measured: doubling the pulse (sdp10 80 → 160, +87 ADC on the pulsed channel) moves the neighbours' pedestal by ~3 ADC (≈ 3.5 %) and σ by +0.3–0.4 ADC; the lower (L) and upper (U) neighbours differ by 1–3 ADC. A σ taken at one amplitude is therefore overestimated. Fix: measure at two amplitudes and extrapolate to zero pulse.

Overestimating σ is not harmless: the threshold is n·σ, so +10 % on σ puts every channel 10 % higher than it needs to be and gives back part of the efficiency we are trying to recover. Underestimating it is worse (noise). Hence two amplitudes, L and U kept apart, and a flag when L and U disagree by more than ~2σ.
Test C · is the pulser σ the real noise?

Sample or peak? Cross-check with a noise-rate scan

Open question: does the neighbour ADC take the baseline at one instant (a true sample → σADC is the noise), or the maximum in a window like the peak detector does for real hits? A maximum of noise is shifted up and narrower, so its width is not the σ that sets the noise rate. The animation shows both readings of the same noise waveform.

The independent check uses only the definition of noise rate: lower the chip threshold in small steps (no pulser, no neighbour read-out), count each channel, fit Rice's curve. ln R is a parabola in the distance d, its curvature gives σ in mV directly. Then per channel k = σmV / σADC: if k is the same for all channels of a chip (one ADC per chip), the pulser σ is validated and the short pulser measurement can be used everywhere. If k follows the σ pattern, the neighbour ADC is biased and the rate scan wins.

Status: the rate scan on chips 2–3 is being analysed now (16 Nov). The right panel shows what each outcome would look like (simulation), not the result.
The algorithm

From one channel to the whole detector

  • Global threshold (sdt, one per chip): sets where the chip sits; must be above the highest target. measured 0.73–0.81 mV/count
  • Per-channel trim (sd, 0..31): pulls each channel down to its own target. measured ~1.1 mV/step
  • σ per channel: pulser + neighbour read-out, with tests A and B built in. chips 2–3 all 18 chips ~20 min
  • σ in mV: rate scan, test C. analysis running
  • Check: measured noise rate per channel against the Rice prediction for its n; outliers raised by the tuner. next
Where efficiency comes back from: (1) masked channels returned to life (276 → 1, done); (2) blind blocks cured (114 → 0, done); (3) quiet channels moved down to their own noise edge instead of the chip's worst one (strategy C, next). (1) and (2) are whole channels; (3) is a few mV on many channels, which matters for the small strip signals at the cluster edges.
