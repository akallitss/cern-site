Does a lower VMM threshold make the P2 chambers more efficient?

Cosmic muons, HV on, a ladder of per-channel thresholds (night 16/17 Nov + day 17 Nov 2026): what we measured, the trap in the obvious plot, and why the answer is about 0.85 whatever the threshold between 14 and 20 mV.

Analysis 2026-11-17 VMM, efficiency, cosmics, P2, threshold, HV

Short answer: no, not in the range we could test. Lowering each channel's threshold from about 20 mV to about 14 mV above its baseline leaves the efficiency where it is, at about 0.85. That is better than the 0.75–0.79 of the 3–5 Nov scan, but still about 0.10 short of the ~0.95 we expect. The muons we miss are not "small signals just under the threshold": those would come back as the threshold goes down, and they do not.

1 · the question

A fence, some noise, and muons

Every pad of a P2 chamber is read by one VMM channel. The channel only records a hit when the signal crosses a threshold, a fence set a few millivolts above the channel's resting level (its baseline). The electronics also produce noise: small random wiggles around the baseline. A low fence lets more muon signals through, but also more noise; a high fence stops the noise, but may stop small muon signals too.

So the natural hope is: lower the fence and the efficiency (the fraction of muons the pad sees) goes up. Whether that is true depends on what the missed muons look like. Play with the toy below: move the fence and switch between two possible worlds.

2 · what we ran

A ladder of thresholds, one rung per run

Instead of one fence height for every channel, each channel got its own fence: the height where its own noise rate, measured the night before with the high voltage on, falls to R hits per second. One configuration file per rung: R = 2, 5, 10, 20 and 50 Hz (100 Hz made too much data for the read-out and was left out). A sixth file, Km18, an older "equalised" file that puts every channel near 18 mV, serves as the reference.

The day ran the ladder up and down (each rung twice, 50 min each) between two Km18 runs; the night before gave four shorter runs. In total 16 runs, about 11 hours of cosmic muons, all with the HV at the low end of the usual range (P2_IN 420 V mesh / 720 V drift, P2_MID and P2_OUT 430 / 730).

Hover a bar for details. Run 233 is struck out: three minutes after it started, the read-out kept sending data but almost no valid hits (a read-out fault reported to the firmware developers), so it is not used.

The fences of each run were not guessed: they were recomputed for every channel from the configuration file that run actually used, with the same calibration that built the files. For the Km18 file this gives back the measured map exactly; for the ladder files the real fence sits 0 to 1 mV above the requested one (the chips set it in steps of about 1.1 mV).

The ladder is narrow: from the quietest to the noisiest rung the median fence moves by only ~4 mV. That is the range this answer covers.
3 · the method, in short

How one pad's efficiency is measured

The method is the one written down on 16 Nov, unchanged; the animated explainer goes through it step by step. In four lines:

  1. Three chambers are stacked. A muon seen by two of them points to where it must have crossed the third.
  2. Only muons that also fired the scintillators above and below are used, so that we know they are muons and not noise that happened to line up.
  3. The pointing is not perfect (3 mm in the middle chamber, 8 mm in the outer ones, for 12 mm pads), so we do not ask "did the pointed pad fire?". We count: how many muons crossed the pad (from the local density of pointed muons), and how many times the pad fired, with random hits removed.
  4. Efficiency = firings / crossings, read off where the count stops depending on the size of the counting circle.
4 · the trap

The obvious plot says "yes". It is lying.

The first plot anyone would make: put every pad of every run into bins of fence height, and compute the efficiency in each bin. Click pooled bins: P2_MID and P2_OUT climb to ~0.95 at the lowest fences. Now click same pads.

Why the two disagree. In the ladder files a channel's fence is set by its own noise. Quiet channels always get a low fence, noisy channels a high one, in every file. So the "low fence" bins are filled mostly with quiet channels, and the "high fence" bins with noisy ones. If the quiet channels also happen to sit on chips that are more efficient, the pooled plot shows a slope that has nothing to do with the fence. It is like ranking running shoes by marathon times when the fastest runners all buy the same brand.

The fair comparison keeps the same pads and changes only the file. That removes the trap. The toy below builds the trap from scratch: 80 pads, two kinds, and no pad changes its efficiency with the fence.

Illustration. Blue pads: quiet and efficient (0.95). Brown pads: noisy and less efficient (0.75). Each rung lowers every fence a little. Grey line: pooled bins over all rungs climb to the left. Black line: the same 80 pads, one point per rung, stay flat. Nothing about any pad changed.

6 · checks

Can we trust it?

  • The method still works on simulated muons, run on the DAQ machine before touching the data: a true efficiency of 0.85 comes back as 0.845–0.849 for pointing errors of 3, 8 and 12 mm.
  • The muons are muons. The time between the scintillator signal and the chamber hits peaks where it should; 99.7 % of the 34 000 muon candidates fall inside the 140–310 ns window used (chart below).
  • Repeat runs agree. Every rung was run twice, hours apart; the two runs agree within their errors, chamber by chamber (chart below). The four Km18 runs (night and day) agree too.
  • Neighbouring pads do not inflate the count much. A muon fires on average 1.04–1.07 pads, so charge shared with a neighbour can push the numbers up by 5 % at most.
  • The counting circle is large enough in P2_MID; in the outer chambers a slightly larger circle adds 0.01–0.02 (muons scattered further), exactly as on 3–5 Nov.
  • Per chip, the numbers are not trustworthy yet: see the next section. The chamber totals do not depend on that, because they agree with the model-free check.

7 · open puzzle

The chips do not agree with each other

Split by VMM chip (each chip reads 64 pads), the same method gives very different numbers: some chips around 0.2–0.5, others above 1, which a real efficiency can never be. These numbers are reproducible (the two runs of each file agree), and a few chips change between files in a way that does not follow the fence (chip 19: 0.43 at R = 2, 0.78 at R = 5, 0.45 at R = 10).

Efficiency per chip (rows) and file (columns) from the counting method. Outlined cells are above 1. Chips 2–7 are P2_IN, 8–13 P2_MID, 14–19 P2_OUT. Treat as a symptom, not a measurement.

The most likely reading: something about where the method thinks some pads are (the map from channel to pad position, pad areas) or how muons are pointed near certain chips is off. Per chip the errors do not cancel; summed over a whole chamber they largely do, which is why the chamber numbers agree with the simple "any pad within 30 mm" check. Next step: hit maps of chips 8 and 12 against the pad map, and the same split for the 3–5 Nov data.

8 · what now

Where the missing 15 % could be

If the misses were small signals just under the fence, lowering the fence by 5 mV would have recovered some of them. It did not, so the misses are mostly muons that give no usable signal at all on that pad, or signals far below any fence we can set. Candidates, and the test for each:

Gain too low
The HV sits at the low end of the usual range. Test: an HV scan with one fixed threshold file. If the efficiency rises with HV, the chambers are gain-limited.
Weak chips or regions
Chips 6, 7, 8 and 19 look low. If that is real, they alone cost 0.05–0.1 per chamber. Test: the hit maps of section 7.
Much lower fences
Below ~12 mV on the same pads needs the noisy channels tamed first (the 100 Hz rung was too much data). Test: closed-loop tuning of the tail, then a lower rung.
9 · operations

Also in this session: the HV ramp-down

At 20:00 the HV guard was meant to ramp all six HV channels down. It switched the first one off and then stopped: the HV supply answers "still on (status 5)" while a channel is ramping down, and the script took that answer for a refusal. Five channels stayed at full voltage until they were switched off by hand at 20:20. The script now accepts that answer, sends the off command again to any channel whose voltage is not falling, and reports loudly if anything is still on after 5 minutes. It is tested with a fake supply that answers exactly as the real one did, and deployed on the DAQ machine.

Technical details (for whoever redoes it)
  • The DAQ machine has 7 GB of RAM, so every run was decoded in pieces of at most 85 MB of capture files (495 pieces, numbered run × 100 + piece), two at a time; every piece then got the dead/masked pad list of its whole run.
  • Fence of every channel in every run: d = dref + (S − Sref)·k − (t − tref)·step, from the run's own configuration copy; references pilot/map_eq.json (ladder files) and fastscan_20261116/full/map_start.json (Km18).
  • Settings: scintillator window 140–310 ns, counting radius 40 mm, pads within 40 mm of a dead pad dropped.
